How much should you charge for house cleaning?

Three pricing models, the numbers behind each, and the cost almost every new cleaner forgets to count.

Almost every new cleaning business starts by copying a competitor's price. It feels safe. It is actually the riskiest thing you can do, because you are adopting someone else's cost structure without knowing what it is.

This guide covers the three models that actually get used, when each one fits, the calculation that decides whether the business survives past month six, and a sample price sheet you can adapt.

The three models

1. Hourly rate

You charge for time: $45/hour × 3 hours = $135. Simple, fair to the customer, and easy to explain. The problem is that hourly pricing punishes speed. Get faster and you earn less per job, which is the opposite of what should happen as you improve.

Hourly works well for: first-time deep cleans, jobs with unknown condition, and any customer who asks "how long will it take?"

2. Flat rate per visit

You quote a fixed price for a defined scope: $150 every two weeks, up to 2,000 sq ft. This is what most recurring residential work uses. It rewards efficiency, makes invoicing predictable, and gives the customer a number they can budget around.

Flat rate fails when the scope is vague. "Standard clean" means different things to you and to the customer. Write the scope down: which rooms, which surfaces, inside appliances or not, how many bathrooms.

3. Room-based

A base price plus per-room adders: $80 base + $25 per bedroom + $30 per bathroom. It is easy to quote over the phone and easy for customers to understand. It undervalues large open-plan homes and overvalues small cluttered ones, so treat it as a starting point and adjust after a walkthrough.

The number almost nobody calculates

Your effective hourly rate is not what you charge. It is what you keep, divided by the hours the job actually consumes — including drive time, setup, supply runs and admin.

Example. A $150 flat-rate clean takes 2.5 hours on site. Drive time is 25 minutes each way. You spend 15 minutes on messages and invoicing.

Total time: 4.1 hours. Effective rate: $36.50/hour — not $60.

Run that number on every job type you offer. The ones that look profitable often are not, and the ones you resent doing usually show up here as a bad effective rate.

Minimum charge and travel fee

Two numbers stop most of the small-job losses:

  • A minimum visit price. Typically the price of your smallest job plus 20%. If a customer only wants one bathroom cleaned, the trip still costs you the same. Without a minimum, you will drive 40 minutes to earn $30.
  • A travel fee outside your core area. Define a radius — 15 or 20 minutes from your base — and charge a flat fee beyond it. State it up front on the quote, not on the invoice.

Both feel awkward to introduce and are normal to customers. Most of them have paid a call-out fee to a plumber before; they understand the concept.

First clean vs recurring: they are different products

A first clean is usually 1.5 to 2.5 times the price of a maintained clean in the same home, because the home has not been cleaned to your standard before. If you quote a first clean at the recurring rate, you will spend six hours on a house you quoted for three.

How to structure it

  • First clean: quoted separately, after a walkthrough or a video call.
  • Recurring clean: priced on the assumption that the home is already maintained.
  • If the customer skips two visits in a row, the next one is priced as a first clean again.

What to add to the base price

  • Supplies: typically 3–6% of revenue. Track it for a month instead of guessing.
  • Insurance: general liability is usually a few hundred dollars a year for a solo cleaner; it is not optional once you are inside someone's home.
  • Drive time: cluster your customers geographically or you will quietly work for free.
  • Payment fees: card processing takes roughly 2.6–3% depending on the provider.
  • Your own tax set-aside: if you are self-employed, this is a real percentage, not a rounding error.

Should you be the cheapest?

No — unless your only differentiator is price, in which case you will be replaced by the next person who is cheaper. Compete on reliability instead:

Same cleaner every visit. A time window you actually keep. A written scope. A photo of the finished kitchen. Those four things beat a $10 discount in almost every customer conversation.

Quoting on the phone without committing

Most first-time calls go like this: the customer describes the home, asks for a price, and expects a number before you hang up. Give a range and a condition:

"For a three-bedroom around that size, most jobs come in between $160 and $200. I confirm the exact price when I see the place — if it's worse than described, I'll call you before I start, not after."

That sentence does three things: it gives a number, it protects you, and it sets the expectation that you communicate before problems become invoices.

When to raise prices

Raise when you are booked more than two weeks out, or when you have not raised prices in a year. Give existing customers 30 days' notice, in writing, with the new price and the date it starts. Expect to lose one or two — that is the cost of the raise, and it is usually smaller than the gain.
