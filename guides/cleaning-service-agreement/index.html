What to put in a cleaning service agreement

The ten sections that prevent most disputes — written in plain English, not legalese.

Not legal advice. This is a practical checklist, not a contract template for your state. Requirements differ (and employee-vs-contractor rules differ a lot). If you are hiring people or signing multi-year commercial work, have a local lawyer review your agreement once — it usually costs a few hundred dollars and lasts years.

Most cleaning disputes are not about cleaning. They are about a gap between what the customer thought they bought and what you thought you sold. An agreement is how you close that gap before the first visit.

The ten sections

1. Who and what

Full legal name of your business, the customer's name, the service address, and the scope in plain language. "Standard cleaning of kitchen, 2 bathrooms, 3 bedrooms" is not a scope. A scope lists surfaces and limits:

Kitchen: counters, sink, stovetop, exterior of appliances, floor. Not included: inside oven, inside fridge, cabinet interiors, dishes.

2. Frequency and duration

Weekly, fortnightly, monthly, one-time. State the expected on-site duration as a range, and say explicitly that it is an estimate, not a guarantee. This one sentence prevents "you said two hours" arguments.

3. Access

How you get in: customer present, key, lockbox, door code, building concierge. If you hold a key, say how it is stored and returned. If the customer is not home, confirm whether you may enter alone. Put the answer in writing — it is the most common source of last-minute cancellations.

4. Supplies and equipment

Who provides what. If you bring supplies, say so and note that the price includes them. If the customer provides, list the minimum required (vacuum with a working filter, mop, cloths) and what happens if they are missing — usually a shortened service at full price, not a discount.

5. Pets and people

Ask about pets that may be loose, and whether anyone will be home. An unmentioned dog is the single most common reason a cleaner leaves without finishing — and then argues about the invoice.

6. Damage and breakage

State that you are insured (if you are), that accidental breakage will be reported the same day, and how it is handled: repair, replacement at depreciated value, or a claim. Do not promise to replace anything at new value — that is an unlimited liability you cannot price.

7. Payment terms

Amount, method, due date, and what happens when payment is late. "Due on completion, by card or bank transfer, with a $25 late fee after 7 days" is enforceable and clear. Vague terms ("payment on invoice") are how small businesses end up chasing money for six weeks.

8. Cancellation and access failure

The window (24 or 48 hours), the fee, and what counts as a failed access. Treat "nobody answered the door" the same way as a late cancellation — otherwise you have built a policy with an obvious loophole. More detail in the deposits guide.

9. Photos and marketing

If you want to use before-and-after photos on your site or social accounts, ask permission in the agreement. Make it opt-in and easy to decline. Using a customer's home in marketing without consent is a genuine privacy problem, not just a faux pas.

10. Ending it

Either side can end a recurring arrangement with X days' notice. No penalty, no awkward phone call. Customers who know they can leave easily are usually the ones who stay.

What not to copy from the internet

  • Templates written for another state. Some clauses (non-compete, contractor classification, liability limits) are unenforceable or illegal in some places.
  • Anything that classifies your cleaners as contractors if you control their schedule, supplies and methods. Misclassification penalties are real and expensive.
  • Unlimited liability language. "We will replace any damaged item at full retail value" is a promise your insurance will not back.

How to use it without scaring customers

Do not send a five-page document before the first clean. Send a one-page summary with the quote — scope, price, access, cancellation window — and say the full agreement is available. Sign the full version once they become a regular.

"I'll send a one-pager with the price and what's included. If you're happy, we start; the longer agreement is for when you become a regular — it protects both of us."
Before your first customer: work out the price with the calculator, then write the scope down in the same sitting. The scope is what the price is attached to.
