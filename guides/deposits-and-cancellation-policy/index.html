Deposits and cancellation policies customers accept

How to protect your calendar without sounding like you distrust everyone.

A no-show does not cost you one job. It costs the slot, the drive, and usually the customer who could have had that slot. For a solo cleaner with four visits a day, one no-show is 25% of the day.

When to take a deposit

SituationDepositWhy
First-time deep clean or move-out clean50%Long job, high material cost, highest no-show rate
Recurring regular, establishedNoneThey have earned the trust; charging now costs goodwill
New recurring customerNone, but card on fileProtects the slot without asking for money up front
Commercial or property-manager workPer contractUsually net-30 terms, not deposits

How much, and what it is for

The deposit should cover the cost of the slot being lost, not the whole job. For a $300 move-out clean, 50% ($150) is defensible; asking for the full amount feels like a scam, and many customers will not pay it to a business they found last week.

Say what it is for, explicitly: "It holds the slot and comes off the final invoice." A deposit that sounds like a fee will be argued about; a deposit that is obviously a prepayment will not.

The cancellation window

24 hours is standard for residential cleaning; 48 is common for deep cleans and move-outs. Shorter than 24 hours and you cannot refill the slot; longer and customers feel trapped by their own calendar.

What counts as a cancellation:

  • Late cancellation — inside the window: charge 50% of the visit price.
  • No-show / no access — nobody home, no key, wrong code: charge the full visit price. You arrived, you were ready, the slot is gone.
  • You cancel — no charge, and offer the next available slot. Your cancellation should cost the customer nothing, or the policy is not a policy, it is a threat.

Phrasing that does not sound hostile

The words matter more than the rule. Compare:

"Cancellations within 24 hours are charged 50%." — reads like a penalty.
"I hold your slot for you, so if something changes give me 24 hours and there's no charge. Inside 24 hours I usually can't fill it, so I charge half." — reads like an explanation.

Same rule. One makes people cancel without telling you; the other makes them call.

Card on file instead of a deposit

For recurring customers, keeping a card on file is often better than a deposit: nothing is charged up front, but a late cancellation can be billed without a negotiation. Two conditions:

  • The agreement must say clearly that the card will be charged for late cancellations or no-access.
  • You must tell the customer when you actually charge it, with the reason and the amount, the same day.

Charging a card silently is how a small business earns a chargeback and a one-star review at the same time.

When a customer disputes the fee

Decide in advance which fights are worth winning. A $40 late fee is not worth a public argument, but the precedent is. A workable line:

"I understand it's frustrating. The fee covers the slot I couldn't fill — I'll waive it this once, and next time I'll need the 24 hours' notice so I can rebook it."

Waive once, state the rule, and log it. If the same customer does it again, the second conversation is much easier because you already told them.

Put the policy where people see it

In the quote, in the one-page summary, and in the agreement. Not buried in a footer. A policy a customer never read is a policy you will lose an argument about.
