Cleaning supplies: what to buy, and what it costs per job

A starter kit under $250, the three things worth paying more for, and how to know your real cost per clean.

Most new cleaners either overspend on a cupboard full of products they never use, or underspend and then lose an hour per job fighting bad equipment. The right answer is narrower than either: a small kit, replaced on a schedule, with the cost per job tracked once so you stop guessing.

What a starter kit actually costs

Prices below are typical US retail ranges for a solo operator doing residential work. A commercial-grade version of the same item often costs 1.5–2× and lasts several times longer — that trade is usually worth it for the items marked below.

ItemTypical costNotes
Microfibre cloths (12–20 pack, colour-coded)$20–$40Buy more than you think. Colour-code by room so you never cross-contaminate.
Mop + 2 mop heads$30–$60Two heads so one can wash while the other works.
Vacuum with HEPA filter$120–$300Spend here. A weak vacuum doubles your floor time and clients notice.
Neutral pH all-purpose cleaner (concentrate)$15–$25Concentrate lasts months; ready-to-use bottles cost 4–6× more per litre.
Bathroom disinfectant + descaler$10–$20Two products, not five. Descaler is what actually removes hard-water marks.
Glass cleaner$8–$15Or one drop of dish soap in water — genuinely works.
Bucket + caddy$15–$30The caddy matters more than the bucket; you carry it room to room.
Gloves, knee pads, apron$25–$45Knee pads are the difference between doing this for two years and five.
Brush set + detail brushes$15–$30Grout, taps, tracks. Cheap brushes are fine.
Trash bags, paper towels, sponges$15–$25Consumable — this is the part that recurs.

Realistic total: $250–$450 for a kit that handles most residential jobs. Not $800, not $80.

Where to spend, where to save

  • Spend: vacuum, mop heads, cloths. These are used every job and their failure costs you time you cannot bill.
  • Save: bottles, buckets, brushes, aprons. There is no meaningful difference between a $4 brush and a $14 brush.
  • Never buy: single-use "cleaning kits" marketed to beginners. You pay for packaging and get a quarter of the quantity.

Your cost per clean

Two numbers decide whether supplies are eating your margin: consumable cost per job, and the replacement cycle of durable items.

Worked example — a 2-bed, 2-bath recurring clean, 3 hours on site.

  • Consumables per job (product, cloths wear, bags, towels): $4.50
  • Durables replaced every 18 months at ~$350: $1.90 per job at 100 jobs/year
  • Total supplies cost: ≈ $6.40 on a $150 job → 4.3% of revenue

That 4–6% band is normal. If you are above 8%, you are either over-buying or under-pricing. If you are at 1%, you are probably not replacing cloths and mop heads often enough — which shows up as complaints, not as savings.

The restock rhythm

Do not restock when you run out; that is how you end up using dish soap on a client's floor at 8am. Restock on a schedule:

  • Weekly: check cloth count, mop head condition, bag stock.
  • Monthly: buy consumables in bulk — one order, one delivery, one invoice.
  • Quarterly: inspect durables. A mop head that smells is not clean, regardless of the product you use.

Who supplies: you or the client?

Bringing your own supplies is usually the better business: you control quality, you know the products, and the price already includes them. Client-supplied products mean you inherit whatever is under their sink.

If a client insists on their own products (allergies, preferences, eco concerns), that is reasonable — but say in the agreement that you are not responsible for results limited by the products provided, and keep your own cloths and vacuum in the car as backup.

Do this once: track supplies for one month — every receipt, in a note on your phone. You will discover your real percentage, and it is almost never the number you assumed.
