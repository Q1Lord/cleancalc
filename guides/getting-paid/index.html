How to get paid: methods, fees, and the 3% problem

What each payment method really costs, when to absorb the fee, and how to set terms that stop you chasing invoices.

A $150 clean paid by card nets you about $145.50. Multiply that by 20 jobs a week and card fees are roughly $470 a month — a real line item that most new cleaners never price in.

The three honest options on fees

  • Absorb it (default). Price your work 3% higher than you would otherwise and never mention fees. Simplest for the customer, cleanest for your brand.
  • Pass it on visibly. "$150 by transfer or cash, $155 by card." Transparent, but it invites a conversation every time.
  • Discount the free methods. "$150 by transfer, $155 by card" is the same as the above — but framed as a discount it lands better with some customers.

What not to do: absorb the fee and then resent it. If it bothers you, price it in once and forget it.

Set terms before the first clean

  • Due on completion for one-off and move-out work. Not "on invoice next week".
  • Card on file for recurring customers, charged the day of service.
  • Late fee clearly stated: "$25 after 7 days". It exists to be rarely used.
  • Deposit for large jobs — see the deposits guide.

Put all four in the quote and the agreement. Terms that only exist in your head are not terms.

The invoice that gets paid fastest

Send it the same day, with four things: the date, the address, the scope in one line, and the amount. One page. A photo of the finished kitchen attached does more for prompt payment than any reminder email.

  • Number every invoice (0142, 0143…). It makes chasing polite and specific.
  • State the due date as a date, not "on receipt".
  • Include one line of payment instructions and nothing else.

When someone does not pay

  1. Day 1 after due: a short, friendly message. Assume it slipped.
  2. Day 7: restate the amount, the due date, and the late fee — once.
  3. Day 14: stop service. Say it plainly and without heat: "I'll pause the next visit until the invoice is settled."

Do not keep cleaning for a customer who has not paid twice. The work you do for them is work you cannot sell to anyone else, and the resentment shows up in the clean.

Keep the paperwork trivial

A spreadsheet with four columns — date, customer, amount, paid yes/no — is enough until you are doing more than about 40 jobs a month. Specialised software earns its fee when chasing invoices becomes the thing you dread, not before.

The number to remember: 3% of revenue. If your prices were set without it, you are working three weeks a year for the payment processor.
