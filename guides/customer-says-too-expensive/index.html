When a customer says "that's too expensive"

Why discounting first is the expensive move, the three questions to ask, and four responses that each fit a different cause.

"That's too expensive" is not a single objection. It is four different objections wearing the same words, and they need different answers. Dropping your price answers none of them — it just tells the customer the original number was invented.

First: do not discount

The instinct is to knock $20 off to keep the job. That instinct costs you twice: once on this job, and again every time that customer refers you as "the one who came down". If the price was right, hold it. If it was wrong, fix it openly — that is a different conversation.

The three questions

  1. "Compared to what?" — a cheaper quote, a previous cleaner, or an assumption? An assumption can be corrected; a comparison needs a scope comparison.
  2. "Which part feels expensive — the total, or what's included?" — the total can be reduced by removing scope; "what's included" is a value problem.
  3. "What were you expecting to spend?" — if the gap is 10%, it is a negotiation. If it is 60%, it is a different customer segment, and no discount closes that.

Four causes, four responses

1. They are comparing to a cheaper quote

"That's fair — can I ask what their quote includes? Often the difference is inside appliances, or whether supplies are included. If they're doing the same list for less, take it; if the list is shorter, I can show you mine next to theirs."

Do not badmouth the competitor. Compare scopes. Half the time the cheaper quote is a shorter list.

2. They assumed a lower number

"That's more than you expected — what number were you working from? If it's the budget that's fixed, I can tell you what fits inside it, and what we'd leave out."

This turns price into scope, which is a conversation you can win honestly.

3. The scope is bigger than they think

"The price reflects two bathrooms and the inside of the oven. If we drop the oven and do the second bathroom every other visit, it comes to $120 — that's a real option, not a discount."

Same revenue per hour, smaller job. This is the only discount that does not cost you margin.

4. They genuinely cannot afford it

"I understand. My price for this scope is $150; I'd rather tell you that than start at $110 and disappear in three months. If that changes, I'm here."

Walking away politely is a business decision, not a failure. A customer who cannot afford your price becomes a customer who complains about every invoice.

What never to do

  • Discount without changing the scope. You have told them the price was arbitrary.
  • Say "I'll see what I can do". It signals the price is soft and invites a counter-offer.
  • Badmouth the cheaper competitor. It makes you look insecure and them look reasonable.
  • Promise extras to compensate. Free add-ons train the customer to expect them.

When to walk away

Walk when the customer wants the full scope at a price below your cost line, or when the negotiation itself takes longer than the job. A useful rule: if the conversation about price has lasted more than two exchanges, the fit is wrong — not the number.
